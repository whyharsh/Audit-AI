Here's what Codex got wrong or missed.

**1. The RoC case cuts against our pitch, not for it.** Codex presents it as evidence of auditor-side pain. Read the actual order: the penalty fell on the company (₹3 lakh) and its Managing Director (₹50,000), not the auditor, and it arose from the company's own suo-moto adjudication application after internal due diligence found the software lacked the edit-log feature. The auditor in that case did their job — they reported the absence in the audit report and were untouched. **2. The perverse incentive nobody has named.** Look at the mechanics of that case again: the auditor's honest 11(g) observation is what created the paper trail that led to the client being fined. Our tool systematically finds edit-log gaps and control exceptions. Every finding is something the partner must now either report (angering the client who pays them) or override on the record. Some partners will see a tool that surfaces problems as a liability generator, not a shield. This is the darkest version of the adoption risk and it must go into the interview script: "if this found a three-week edit-log gap at your biggest client, what would you do?" The answer to that question decides the company more than the willingness-to-pay question does.

**3. The liability boomerang on the seal itself.** Codex accepts the sealed trail as pure upside. It isn't. A tamper-evident file that shows the partner saw exception X, wrote a thin override reason, and signed anyway is more damning in an inspection than today's vague Excel file. Defensibility cuts both ways: we're selling proof of diligence, but we're also manufacturing proof of negligence for anyone who uses it carelessly. The product answer is that override quality becomes a first-class feature (guided reasoning, materiality justification templates), but the positioning answer is subtler and untested: partners may intuit this risk and hesitate.

**4. The seal has no official verifier.** ICAI peer reviewers and NFRA inspectors work to SA 230 documentation standards, which are professional standards, not cryptographic ones. Nobody at ICAI recomputes Merkle roots. So the seal's value today is persuasive (this file is demonstrably untouched) rather than procedural (the inspector's checklist requires it). That's still worth something, but Codex treats the seal as the product's core when its near-term value is mostly narrative. The procedurally required things are humbler: complete documentation, evidenced procedures, timely assembly (SQC 1's 60-day file assembly rule is actually a nice hook nobody's mentioned). Don't let the crypto romance outrun what inspectors actually check.

The real market sizing is mid-tier firms times audits-per-season times a price that survives, which is exactly the unresolved ACV question, and citing big MCA numbers papers over it.
